⚡ 1. The Quantum Threat to Classical Cryptography
KEY IDEA: Quantum computers running Shor's algorithm can break RSA, Diffie-Hellman, and ECC in polynomial time. This threatens all public-key cryptography currently in use.

Shor's Algorithm (1994)

What it breaks: Integer factorization and discrete logarithm problems -- the mathematical foundations of RSA, Diffie-Hellman, and Elliptic Curve Cryptography (ECC). A sufficiently powerful quantum computer makes all of these insecure.

Grover's Algorithm (1996)

What it affects: Provides a quadratic speedup for searching unstructured databases. For symmetric cryptography, this halves the effective key length:
AlgorithmClassical SecurityPost-Quantum SecurityAction Needed
AES-128128-bit64-bit (broken)Migrate to AES-256
AES-256256-bit128-bit (safe)Already quantum-safe
SHA-256256-bit128-bit (safe)Already quantum-safe
Grover's vs Shor's: Grover's is a speedup (fix by doubling key size). Shor's is a complete break (must switch to entirely new algorithms). The asymmetric crypto problem is far more urgent.
Remember This
Shor = Breaks public-key (RSA, DH, ECC). Grover = Weakens symmetric (double key sizes).
🔒 2. Harvest Now, Decrypt Later
KEY IDEA: Adversaries are capturing encrypted data today with the expectation of decrypting it once quantum computers become available. Data with long confidentiality requirements is at immediate risk.
Adversary intercepts encrypted traffic today
➔
Stores ciphertext for years
➔

Decrypts all stored data using Shor's algorithm
Why this matters NOW: Government secrets, medical records, financial data, and intellectual property may need to remain confidential for 20-50+ years. If quantum computers arrive in 10-15 years, data encrypted today with RSA/ECC is already at risk.
Implication: Organizations with long-lived sensitive data must begin migrating to post-quantum cryptography now, not when quantum computers arrive. The migration timeline is the critical factor.
🏛️ 3. NIST Post-Quantum Cryptography Standardization
NIST PQC Competition: Started in 2016 with 69 submissions. Multi-round evaluation process leading to final standards published in 2024.

The Three NIST PQC Standards

StandardAlgorithmTypeUse CaseApproach
FIPS 203 ML-KEM (Kyber) Key Encapsulation Key exchange / encryption Lattice-based (Module-LWE)
FIPS 204 ML-DSA (Dilithium) Digital Signature Authentication / signing Lattice-based (Module-LWE)
FIPS 205 SLH-DSA (SPHINCS+) Digital Signature Authentication / signing Hash-based (stateless)
Why three standards? ML-KEM handles key exchange (replacing ECDH). ML-DSA is the primary signature algorithm (replacing ECDSA/RSA signatures). SLH-DSA is a conservative backup -- its security relies only on hash functions, making it the safest bet if lattice assumptions fail.
Remember This
203 = KEM (Kyber, keys) | 204 = DSA (Dilithium, signatures) | 205 = SLH-DSA (SPHINCS+, hash backup)
🔬 4. Lattice-Based Cryptography
Learning With Errors (LWE): The core hard problem. Given a system of approximate linear equations over a finite field, find the secret vector. The "errors" (noise) make the problem computationally hard -- even for quantum computers.

How Lattice Crypto Works

The Lattice Problem

  • Lattice = a regular grid of points in high-dimensional space
  • Hard problem: Finding the closest vector to a target point (CVP) or the shortest non-zero vector (SVP)
  • In high dimensions (hundreds to thousands), these problems are believed to be hard for both classical and quantum computers
  • No known quantum speedup -- unlike factoring or discrete log
Lattice Problem (Simplified 2D View)
* * * * * * * * * * * * * * X * * * X = target point * * * * * Find the closest lattice * * * * * * point to X (easy in 2D, * * * * * extremely hard in 500+ dimensions) * * * * * *
Why lattices won the NIST competition: Good performance (small keys, fast operations relative to other PQC families), strong security reductions, and decades of cryptanalysis without a quantum attack.
🛡️ 5. Hash-Based Signatures
Hash-based signatures derive their security entirely from hash function properties (collision resistance, preimage resistance). This makes them the most conservative PQC choice -- if the hash function is secure, the signature scheme is secure.

SPHINCS+ (SLH-DSA / FIPS 205)

  • Stateless -- no need to track which keys have been used (unlike earlier hash-based schemes like XMSS)
  • Very conservative security -- relies only on hash functions
  • Trade-off: Larger signatures compared to lattice-based schemes
  • Acts as a backup standard in case lattice-based assumptions are broken
Stateful vs Stateless: Earlier hash-based schemes (Lamport, Merkle, XMSS) were stateful -- reusing a one-time key compromises security. SPHINCS+ solves this by being stateless, but at the cost of larger signatures.
📜 6. Code-Based and Multivariate Cryptography

Code-Based Cryptography

McEliece Cryptosystem (1978): One of the oldest PQC candidates. Based on the difficulty of decoding a general linear error-correcting code. Has survived 45+ years of cryptanalysis.

McEliece Properties

  • Very fast encryption and decryption
  • Large key sizes -- public keys can be hundreds of kilobytes to megabytes
  • Still a NIST Round 4 candidate (Classic McEliece)
  • Based on Goppa codes and the hardness of general decoding

Multivariate Cryptography

Core problem: Solving systems of multivariate quadratic polynomial equations over finite fields. This is NP-hard in general and has no known quantum speedup.
Status: NIST selected Rainbow (a multivariate scheme) for signatures, but it was broken by a classical attack in 2022. This highlights the risk of newer PQC families and the importance of conservative backup standards like SPHINCS+.
🔑 7. Zero-Knowledge Proofs (ZKPs)
KEY IDEA: A zero-knowledge proof lets a prover convince a verifier that a statement is true without revealing any information beyond the truth of the statement itself.

Three Properties of ZKPs

PropertyMeaning
CompletenessIf the statement is true, an honest prover can always convince an honest verifier
SoundnessA cheating prover cannot convince the verifier of a false statement (except with negligible probability)
Zero-KnowledgeThe verifier learns nothing beyond the fact that the statement is true

Interactive vs Non-Interactive

Types of ZKPs

  • Interactive ZKPs: Prover and verifier exchange multiple messages back and forth. The verifier sends challenges, the prover responds.
  • Non-Interactive ZKPs (NIZKs): The prover generates a single proof that anyone can verify. No back-and-forth required. Essential for blockchain applications.
  • Fiat-Shamir heuristic: Converts interactive proofs to non-interactive by replacing verifier challenges with hash function outputs.
Remember This
ZKP = "I know the secret" without saying what the secret is. Complete, Sound, Zero-Knowledge.
💎 8. ZK-SNARKs -- Succinct Non-interactive Arguments of Knowledge
ZK-SNARK = Zero-Knowledge Succinct Non-interactive Argument of Knowledge. Produces very small, fast-to-verify proofs but requires a trusted setup phase.

Key Properties

  • Succinct: Proofs are very small (a few hundred bytes) regardless of computation complexity
  • Non-interactive: Single message from prover to verifier
  • Argument of Knowledge: Proves the prover actually knows the witness (not just that it exists)
  • Trusted setup required: A one-time ceremony generates public parameters. If the setup is compromised, fake proofs can be created.
Trusted Setup Risk: The "toxic waste" (secret randomness) from the setup ceremony must be destroyed. If any participant retains it, they can forge proofs. This is a single point of failure.
Real-world use: ZK-SNARKs power Zcash cryptocurrency, enabling private transactions where the amount and participants are hidden but the transaction validity is publicly verifiable.
⭐ 9. ZK-STARKs -- Scalable Transparent Arguments of Knowledge
KEY IDEA: ZK-STARKs are the quantum-resistant evolution of zero-knowledge proofs. No trusted setup (transparent), hash-based security, and better scalability for large computations.
ZK-STARK = Zero-Knowledge Scalable Transparent ARgument of Knowledge. Invented by Eli Ben-Sasson and collaborators (2018).

Key Properties

  • Transparent: No trusted setup -- all randomness is publicly verifiable (derived from hash functions)
  • Quantum-resistant: Security relies only on collision-resistant hash functions, not on elliptic curves or pairings
  • Scalable: Verification time grows quasi-logarithmically with computation size -- scales better for large computations
  • Larger proofs: Proofs are bigger than SNARKs (tens to hundreds of KB vs hundreds of bytes)

ZK-STARKs vs ZK-SNARKs

FeatureZK-SNARKsZK-STARKs
SetupTrusted setup requiredTransparent (no trusted setup)
Proof SizeSmaller (~200-300 bytes)Larger (~10-100 KB)
Quantum ResistanceNo (relies on elliptic curves)Yes (relies on hash functions)
Verification SpeedVery fast (constant time)Fast (quasi-logarithmic)
ScalabilityLess scalable for large computationsMore scalable (quasi-linear prover)
Cryptographic BasisElliptic curve pairingsCollision-resistant hash functions
Example UseZcashStarkNet, StarkEx
Why STARKs matter for PQC: Because STARKs are hash-based, they survive the quantum transition. SNARKs rely on elliptic curve pairings that Shor's algorithm can break. In a post-quantum world, STARKs are the clear choice for zero-knowledge proofs.
Remember This
SNARK = Small proofs, Need trusted setup, NOT quantum-safe. STARK = Scalable, Transparent, quantum-safe (hash-based).
🔄 10. Crypto Agility and Hybrid Approaches
KEY IDEA: Crypto agility means designing systems so cryptographic algorithms can be swapped without major redesign. This is essential for surviving the PQC transition and future algorithm breaks.

Crypto Agility Principles

Design for Algorithm Replacement

  • Abstract crypto interfaces: Don't hardcode specific algorithms -- use pluggable modules
  • Algorithm negotiation: Protocols should support multiple algorithms and negotiate at runtime (like TLS cipher suites)
  • Key management flexibility: Support multiple key types and sizes simultaneously
  • Inventory your crypto: Know where every algorithm is used across your systems

Hybrid Approaches

Hybrid key exchange: Run a classical algorithm (e.g., ECDH) and a PQC algorithm (e.g., ML-KEM) simultaneously. Combine both shared secrets. Security is at least as strong as the stronger algorithm.

Why hybrid? We trust classical algorithms through years of cryptanalysis, but they fall to quantum. We believe PQC algorithms are secure, but they're newer and less battle-tested. Hybrid gives you the best of both during the transition.
🚧 11. PQC Migration Challenges
Migration is not just a crypto swap. Moving to post-quantum cryptography involves systemic challenges across hardware, software, protocols, and organizations.
ChallengeDetails
Larger Key SizesPQC public keys and signatures are significantly larger. ML-KEM public key ~1.5 KB vs ECDH ~32 bytes. Impacts bandwidth, storage, and protocol overhead.
Embedded / IoT DevicesResource-constrained devices may lack memory, compute power, or update mechanisms for PQC algorithms.
Backward CompatibilitySystems must communicate with both PQC and non-PQC endpoints during transition. Protocol versioning is critical.
Performance ImpactSome PQC operations are slower. Key generation, signing, and verification may have different performance profiles.
Certificate InfrastructureX.509 certificates, PKI hierarchies, and certificate transparency logs must accommodate larger PQC keys and signatures.
Organizational ReadinessTeams need training, crypto inventories, migration roadmaps, and testing infrastructure.
